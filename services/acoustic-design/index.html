A few things worth understanding before you buy anything

Loud is not the same as clear

Most complaints about gym, cafeteria, and field systems aren't about volume. They're about intelligibility: the sound arrives, but the words don't. That's a function of loudspeaker placement, coverage pattern, zoning, and the room itself. Adding power to a badly placed system makes it louder and no clearer. Design fixes it; bigger amplifiers usually don't.

Sound masking, briefly

Sound masking is not noise cancellation. It's a carefully shaped, barely noticeable background sound tuned to the range of human speech. Raising that background a little in the right bands reduces how far a conversation stays intelligible, which can improve speech privacy and reduce distraction when the system is appropriately designed and implemented. It doesn't isolate sound or make a conversation confidential, and it shouldn't be sold as if it did. Done well, nobody notices it. Done badly, people complain about a hiss and turn it off. The difference is in the design and the commissioning targets, which is why those are specified rather than left to the installer's defaults.

Direct-field or in-plenum

Masking can be delivered by emitters facing down through the ceiling tile, or by loudspeakers firing up into the plenum so the sound filters down. Each is right for different ceiling types, plenum depths, HVAC layouts, and budgets. We design both and recommend the one your building wants.

Treatment is a different job

If the problem is echo and reverberation, a loud room where sound hangs in the air, that's acoustic treatment: absorptive panels and clouds that change how the room behaves. Masking won't fix it, and treatment won't give you speech privacy. We'll be clear about which problem you have.

How an engagement runs

  1. Scoping. You describe the space and the problem; floor plans and photos help if you have them. We agree a written scope and fee.
  2. Design or review. You receive the deliverables defined in that scope, explained in plain language with the reasoning shown.
  3. Questions. You, your supplier, or your installer can come back with questions on the design within the scope.
  4. Handoff. You take the design to the suppliers and installers you choose. If commissioning criteria are part of the scope, they're written so the installer can verify the result.
